Why Does My Pool Lose More Water When the Pump Is Running?

When water loss is worse with the pump on, the leak is on the pressure side of your plumbing. The pump is actively pushing water out through the failure point faster.

Direct Answer

A pool that loses more water when the pump is running has a pressure-side leak. The pump pushes water through the return lines under pressure. If there is a crack, split, or failed fitting on the return lines, equipment connections, or anything after the pump, the pump is actively forcing water out through that failure every time it runs. The leak is not in the pool shell — it is in the plumbing. A licensed pool leak specialist can pressure test the lines to confirm exactly which one is failing.

Understanding the Two Sides of Pool Plumbing

Every pool plumbing system has two distinct sides, and knowing which side the leak is on tells you a great deal before a single test is run.

Suction Side — Before the Pump

  • Skimmer lines
  • Main drain lines
  • Everything from the pool to the pump inlet
  • Pulls water toward the pump under negative pressure
  • A suction-side leak often draws air in rather than leaking water out

Pressure Side — After the Pump

  • Filter, heater, salt system connections
  • All return lines from equipment back to the pool
  • Return fittings at the pool wall
  • Water features, deck jets, spa jets
  • Pushes water under positive pressure — leaks push out

Why the Pump Makes a Pressure-Side Leak Worse

When the pump is off, the plumbing system has no active pressure. Water may still seep out of a crack through gravity if the leak is below the waterline, but the rate is relatively slow.

When the pump turns on, it builds pressure throughout the return side — typically between 15 and 30 PSI depending on the equipment and filter condition. That pressure is constantly pushing water through every fitting, every glue joint, every union, and every inch of return pipe. If any of those connections have failed, the pump is now actively working against you — forcing water out through the failure at a much higher rate than gravity alone would produce.

This is why a pool owner sometimes notices the water level is fine in the morning but drops significantly during the pump cycle — then partially stabilizes overnight.

Common Pressure-Side Leak Sources

  • Underground return lines — PVC return pipes running under the deck or yard that have cracked from soil movement, root intrusion, or age.
  • Return wall fittings — the eyeball fittings where water enters the pool. Gaskets and o-rings fail over time, especially in older pools.
  • Equipment pad connections — unions, fittings, and valves at the filter, heater, and salt system can weep or fail, especially after filter backwashing or equipment maintenance.
  • Water feature lines — waterfalls, deck jets, spa jets, and bubblers run on the pressure side and can develop cracks in their supply lines underground.
  • Check valves — a failed check valve between the spa and pool can allow the pressure side to drain the spa or allow water to bypass equipment.

The Pump On/Off Test — How It Works

A simple way to confirm a pressure-side leak before calling for professional testing: run the pool for 30 minutes with the pump on, then mark the water level. Turn the pump off and mark the same level again after 30 minutes. If the water drops noticeably faster with the pump running, you have confirmed pressure-side involvement. This observation tells a leak detection technician exactly where to start looking.

What If the Pool Loses Water Only When the Pump Is Off?

The opposite pattern — water loss that happens when the pump is off and slows or stops when it runs — points to structural or gravity-driven leaks rather than pressure-side plumbing. These include cracks in the pool shell at or below the waterline, a leaking skimmer throat, a pool light conduit that allows water to drain out, or return fittings where the check valve or plug is missing. The pool loses water by gravity whenever the pump is not pressurizing the system.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I turn off certain features to narrow down which line is leaking?

Yes. If you have a spa, waterfall, or deck jets on separate valves, turning them off one at a time while watching the water level can help narrow which line is involved. If the water loss stops or slows when a specific feature is turned off, that circuit is likely where the leak is. Report those observations when you call — it helps us prioritize which lines to test first.

Does a pressure-side leak mean I have to dig up my deck?

Not necessarily. Professional leak detection locates the leak first — before any digging. We use pressure testing to confirm which line is failing, then acoustic equipment or trace gas to narrow the exact location underground. Excavation only happens once the location is confirmed, and it is targeted — not exploratory.

How do you pressure test pool return lines?

We isolate individual plumbing circuits using test plugs, then introduce controlled air or water pressure into the isolated section. A line that holds pressure is intact. A line that drops pressure is leaking. We test each circuit systematically until we find which one is failing, then use acoustic tools to locate where on that line the failure is.

What if the pool loses water both with the pump on and off?

Both patterns at once usually means there are two separate leaks — one structural and one plumbing — or a single large leak that loses water by gravity and by pressure both. That is common on pools that have been leaking undetected for a long time. We test both sides systematically and document every source we find in one visit.
