What Is Trace Gas Testing for Pool Leaks?

When a pool pipe leaks underground and acoustic equipment cannot pinpoint exactly where, trace gas can. A safe hydrogen-nitrogen blend follows the leak path through soil so excavation happens in the right place the first time.

Direct Answer

Trace gas testing injects a safe blend of 5% hydrogen and 95% nitrogen into an isolated pool plumbing line that has already been confirmed leaking by pressure testing. The gas escapes through the leak point in the pipe and rises vertically through the soil above it. A sensitive electronic sniffer probe sweeps the surface — deck, pavers, grass — following the gas signal to pinpoint where the underground leak is located. This allows precise excavation at the right spot without exploratory digging. The gas blend is non-flammable and non-toxic at the concentration used.

Why Trace Gas Is Needed for Underground Pool Leaks

Pressure testing confirms that a plumbing circuit is leaking — but it does not tell you where on that circuit the failure is. The pipe could be 10 feet long or 80 feet long, running under a concrete deck, through soil, and back to the equipment pad.

Acoustic listening equipment can often narrow the location by detecting the sound of water or air escaping from the pipe. But sound does not travel equally well through all conditions. Heavy concrete overlays, deep pipes, saturated soil, and nearby noise sources can all reduce acoustic signal clarity to the point where the technician cannot pinpoint the leak with confidence.

Trace gas molecules are extremely small — smaller than water or air — and they follow the path of least resistance upward through soil. Where the gas exits the surface is directly above where the pipe is leaking. The signal is independent of pipe depth or concrete thickness in a way that acoustic listening is not.

How Trace Gas Testing Works — Step by Step

  1. Pressure test confirms the leaking line

    Before trace gas is used, the specific plumbing circuit must be confirmed leaking by pressure testing. Trace gas is a locating tool — not a detection tool. It is used after the failing line is identified.

  2. The circuit is isolated and plugged

    All ends of the plumbing circuit are plugged to contain the gas within that line. A proper seal is critical — any loose plug contaminates the surface environment and ruins the test.

  3. Gas is injected from the farthest practical point

    The tank is positioned as far from the suspected break area as possible — ideally downwind — to avoid contaminating the surface near the locate zone before the gas has time to travel through the pipe and exit at the leak point.

  4. Pressure is set and held below 20 PSI

    Low pressure is maintained — typically well below 20 PSI. Large breaks need less pressure. Small breaks may need more, up to the 20 PSI maximum. Overpressuring compromises the test and risks the pipe.

  5. The sniffer follows the pipe route

    The technician sweeps the surface sniffer along the known pipe route, holding the probe at the surface with proper dwell time to allow the instrument to register. Speed matters — moving too fast misses the signal.

  6. The strongest sustained signal is the locate

    The probe response rises near the leak and peaks directly above it. The strongest and longest-lasting reading — not just the first spike — is used to confirm the location before marking.

Trace Gas vs. Acoustic Listening — When Each Is Used

Acoustic listening

Best when the pipe is at moderate depth, soil is not heavily saturated, and ambient noise is manageable. Faster to set up and can be used earlier in the process. May struggle with deep pipes, thick concrete slabs, or saturated South Florida soil after rain.

Trace gas

Best when acoustic signals are ambiguous or the pipe is deep under heavy concrete. Gas molecules are smaller than sound waves and rise through soil more consistently. Takes longer to set up but produces a clearer locate in difficult conditions. Always used after pressure testing confirms the circuit.

Trace gas is the tool that lets us tell a homeowner exactly where to cut the deck before we ever pick up a chisel. Without it, on a deep pipe under travertine or concrete, we would be estimating a range and hoping. With it, we can mark a spot and have confidence we are within a foot of the actual break.

The discipline with trace gas is in the setup. If the plugs are not fully seated before you inject, you contaminate the surface and the test is done. The protocol is everything — you inject from the far end, you stay downwind, you give the gas time to travel, and you move the sniffer slowly. Rush any of those steps and you get a false reading that puts the excavation in the wrong place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the trace gas harmful to the pool, soil, or plants?

No. The 5% hydrogen / 95% nitrogen blend is non-toxic and does not harm pool water, soil chemistry, or surrounding plants. The gas dissipates quickly once it exits the soil.

Can trace gas find a leak under a concrete pool deck?

Yes. Gas molecules are small enough to pass through micro-gaps in concrete, expansion joints, and paver seams. The signal from trace gas is less affected by concrete thickness than acoustic sound, making it particularly useful under heavy deck surfaces common in South Florida.

Does trace gas work in wet or saturated soil?

Saturated soil does slow gas migration but does not eliminate it. In some cases we allow more dwell time and reduce the sniffer sweep speed to account for wet conditions. Trace gas is generally more reliable than acoustic listening in saturated South Florida soil after heavy rain.

How accurate is trace gas for locating an underground pool leak?

Under good conditions, trace gas can locate an underground pipe leak within one to two feet of the actual failure point. Combined with pipe locating equipment to confirm the pipe route, this allows targeted excavation rather than exploratory digging across a broad area.
